    Common Ground

    Seven scriptures, one map: every verse placed by what it says. A language model read each verse and gave it a place, so that verses which say similar things sit close together. That space was flattened to a map, and the map raised into ground whose height is how many verses stand there. Where the colours meet, the traditions agree. Where one colour stands alone, a tradition speaks for itself.

    What the numbers mean

    Shared ground asks how many of a verse's fifteen nearest neighbours belong to other texts, against what chance would give its own text, so a small text is not called shared just for being small. It describes the verse, not any pair. Nearest in each other text is the closest verse by meaning in each of the six other texts, however far away. The bar shows how close the pair is, in fifths set against every such pair in the corpus; the farthest fifth are left out, so a verse that keeps its own company may list only a few. Search by meaning reads what you type with the same model, in your browser, and scores every verse against it. The model is fetched once, and nothing you type leaves the page.

    What to be careful of

    These are English translations, so part of the map belongs to the translators. The units differ: a Bible verse, an aya of the Quran, a stanza of the Gita, a chapter of the Analects. The model is small and the layout is one run of one algorithm. Closeness of meaning is not agreement, and a nearest verse is not a matching one. Treat what you find as a place to start reading, not a conclusion.
